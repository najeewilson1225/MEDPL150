Thoughts & Perspectives

Thought 01 — Beyond the Eye

Before reading this excerpt from Susan Sontag, I viewed taking photos as capturing moments in the present for future memories. If I like the aesthetics around me or want to cherish an experience, I simply take a picture. Most of the time, I don't think much beyond that. However, after reading Sontag, I began thinking that taking a photograph is more than just pressing a button to capture a moment. The reason why you chose to capture that specific moment matters, along with what you chose not to capture and how other people may interpret it. Looking through old pictures can bring back perspectives and emotions that I may not have felt in the moment. A picture can remind me of where I was, who I was around, and even how I felt during that time. At the same time, Sontag made me wonder whether we sometimes focus so much on capturing a moment that we aren't fully living in it. I also realized that photography can preserve things that may eventually change or disappear. This makes me think differently about everyday pictures because something that seems ordinary to me now could become meaningful years later.

I think photography is even more relevant today with social media and how vastly technology is evolving. Moreover, I believe the person behind the camera controls what the viewer gets to see. This can sway a person's perspective depending on angles and lighting. I could take several pictures of the same place, giving off a completely different feeling depending on the compositional elements. Furthermore, editing adds another layer to that. Something as simple as changing the brightness or cropping part of an image can change one's perspective. We are constantly viewing images of people's lives, but we only see what they want us to see. Someone's life can look perfect through a lens, while reality can be totally skewed. I think it would be interesting to create images that explore those different views. I could photograph something that appears one way at first and combine it with text that gives the viewer my perspective. The viewer may not have seen what I envisioned and could interpret it completely differently. I like the idea of making someone look or think twice instead of immediately understanding everything.

As I complete the projects this semester, I want to look at them as a collective instead of separate assignments. What I learn about composition in one project could affect how I use lighting in another. This will not only show my growth but also allow me to be more diverse. It can also expose me to different art forms and techniques by learning from my peers. By the end of the semester, I want to be able to look at my work and see not only improvement in my technical skills, but also a better understanding of what I am trying to communicate through an image. I want to learn how to not only take a picture but also be able to explain the emotions and thoughts behind the reasoning of the photo.

Thought 02 — Who's Thinking?

Before reviewing the reading and video, I never really considered how using AI could affect how much information I actually remember or process myself. I have always viewed AI as a tool, and after learning more about its possible effects, I still view it that way. However, I now understand that there is more nuance to how we use these tools and how our brains respond to them. Our brains have to be exercised in order to continue growing, just like other skills we develop. To me, giving someone access to AI without teaching them how to properly use it is like giving someone a screwdriver and telling them to hammer a nail into the wall. The tool itself is not necessarily the problem. The problem is not knowing how to use it.

One finding from the reading that stood out to me was the difference between the Brain-only, Search Engine, and LLM groups. The Brain-only group showed the strongest neural connectivity, while the LLM group showed the weakest overall connectivity. What interested me even more was that participants using LLMs had difficulty quoting from essays they had written only minutes before and reported less ownership of their work. I can wholeheartedly understand how this could happen. If AI writes something for you, it would naturally be harder to explain because those aren't your authentic thoughts. AI can also strip your voice from the writing through vocabulary, tone, or expressions you wouldn't normally use. At that point, people wonder how much of the assignment actually belongs to you.

The video also made me think more about the difference between using AI and allowing AI to think for you. I personally use AI, but I do not tell it to complete my assignments for me. I usually start by giving it my immediate thoughts and then ask for help organizing them into an outline, steps, or a checklist. For me, this makes completing a task more efficient and helps ensure that I am addressing all of the requirements without getting sidetracked. I do not believe that kind of assistance automatically creates dependency. Instead, it allows me to spend more of my attention on the parts of the assignment that I consider important. There is a major difference between having a tool organize your thoughts and allowing that tool to create your thoughts.

I also agree with the researchers being cautious about claiming that AI makes people "dumber." I do not think that is the point. The reading and video instead reminded me that I should not allow AI to become me or my brain. My concern is not simply the information AI produces, but how disconnected or mentally lazy someone could become if they continuously allow a robot to solely think for them. Moreover, I believe schools should incorporate AI into the curriculum and teach students how to adequately use it. AI is becoming part of almost everything we do, so avoiding it completely does not make sense to me. Teaching people how to use it from the beginning could instead prevent misuse. Ultimately, AI can be a useful tool, but we should not give complete trust or responsibility for our thinking to something that is not always certain itself.

Thought 03 — The Other Side of Grief

I chose Kadiatu's story, "The Devil at My Door," from Motherhood in Crisis because the title immediately intrigued me. The suspense of not knowing who or what the devil was made me want to continue reading. As I read her story, I was filled with sorrow for all the pain and suffering she had endured. I began to feel especially sympathetic toward her because it seemed as though she simply could not catch a break. I interpreted the devil at her door as a representation of the repeated tragedies and hardships she experienced throughout her life. Throughout the story, I noticed that none of the illustrated characters smiled. Their facial expressions appeared sad, stressed, or angry. The illustrator used gloomy and dull colors to emphasize the distress and sadness Kadiatu experienced. I also noticed the details of the wrinkles in the characters' faces and clothing, which suggested exhaustion and the emotional weight they carried. I believe the illustrator chose these colors intentionally because using bright colors would not have communicated the same message of grief and suffering.

One illustration that stood out to me was when Kadiatu was looking at her children's graves. The use of color and the wave-like appearance of the background caught my attention. It almost looked as though a glare was shining on her, yet the overall atmosphere remained gloomy, representing the grief she was feeling. Another illustration that affected me was the image of her son suffering. The tears on his face emphasized his pain and made the scene more emotional. I believe these small artistic details matter because they allowed me to visualize the suffering that Kadiatu and her family experienced. I believe illustrations were used because they give the artist more freedom to express emotions that may be difficult to capture through photography alone. It is also possible that photographs were not available during some of Kadiatu's traumatic experiences. The illustrations allowed the artist to recreate those moments while emphasizing the emotions surrounding them. Unlike the illustrations, the real photographs at the end showed another side of Kadiatu's life. Seeing her children, including Abdul, looking happy made me smile after reading such a heartbreaking story. To me, the photographs represented hope and resilience. They made me feel as though the devil was no longer at her door, even though she had experienced so much suffering. The photographs also created a personal connection because I could finally see the real people behind the illustrated characters and the experiences they had endured.

Overall, I believe combining illustrations, photographs, and text made Kadiatu's story more powerful. If the story had been presented only through text, I might have focused entirely on her suffering and believed the devil was still at her door. The illustrations helped me understand the emotional weight of her experiences, while the photographs allowed me to recognize how much she had overcome. Together, these visual elements showed not only her grief but also her strength and determination to continue living for her children.
